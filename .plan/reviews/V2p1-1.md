## Findings

### F1 (BLOCKING) — the inbound causation chain forks under concurrent arrival, and the stream then drains out of order

- **File:** `backend/src/return_platform/operations/return_support/ingress_store.py`
- **Lines:** 320–329 (`_classify_command` awaited **before** `with_transaction`), 458–465 (`_last_enqueued_inbound_event` + `ordered_command_fields` called with **no** `session`), 487–503.
- **Rules violated:** contract §7 ("per-case streams … dispatch only when predecessors complete"), acceptance 18 (inbound-stream ordered drain); charter standard dimensions (concurrency on concurrent case writes).
- **What happens.** `record_inbound_message` resolves the chain tail and allocates the stream sequence *outside* its transaction, then commits. Two ingress calls in flight on the same case therefore both read the same tail and both name it as their predecessor. The result is a **fork**, not a chain: the sequence numbers are still distinct (S2's `$inc` is server-side and correct), but two events sit at the same depth. Once their common predecessor delivers, both become dispatchable simultaneously and the dispatcher is free to run them in either order — which is precisely what `required_predecessor_ids` exists to prevent.
- **Confirmed by RV, two probes, both reverted.**
  1. Modelling two overlapping requests (letting the first request's tail read yield before its commit — the ordinary interleaving of two HTTP calls): predecessors came back `[[], [ev1], [ev1]]`. Two events, one predecessor, **forked**.
  2. Driving that forked chain through **S2's real `IntegrationOutboxDispatcher`**, with the queue loaded against the answer exactly as the branch's own acceptance-18 test does: chain order by `streamSequence` was `[ev1, ev3, ev2]`; **delivered order was `[ev1, ev2, ev3]`**. The message with the later stream sequence was delivered before the one with the earlier sequence. The ordered drain does not hold.
- **Why the branch's own proof did not catch it.** `test_the_dispatcher_drains_the_inbound_stream_in_order` is a genuinely strong test — see the section below — but it records its three messages **sequentially** (`for identifier in ("m-1","m-2","m-3"): await _record(...)`), so it builds a well-formed chain and then proves the dispatcher honours it. It proves the *dispatcher* half. The *enqueuing* half — that the store always produces a chain rather than a fork — is the half that is unproven, and it is the half §7 assigns to the enqueuing store ("populated only by the enqueuing store from the causation chain").
- **Why it matters beyond the test.** Support messages arriving close together on one case is ordinary, not exotic: a transport delivering a burst, an agent sending two lines in quick succession, or a redelivery overlapping a fresh arrival. Out-of-order inbound analysis means a later message's extraction can commit before an earlier one's — e.g. a correction landing before the thing it corrects.
- **Fix.** S2 already supports the correct shape and V2 did not use it: `ordered_command_fields(..., session=...)` takes a session, and S2's own `DurableCaseCommandStore.plan_command` passes it. Move the tail read and the sequence allocation inside the transaction and pass the session through. Deriving the predecessor from the allocated `streamSequence - 1` within the same allocation would close it more tightly still, since it removes the read-then-write entirely. Either way, add a concurrent-arrival case to the chain test — the sequential one should stay.

### F2 (rule 10) — the entry-id collision tests pass on the separator, not on the length prefixes they claim to pin

- **Files:** `backend/tests/operations/test_support_relay_and_wiring.py:195-209` (`test_the_entry_id_cannot_collide_across_a_shifted_boundary`) and the corresponding case for `derive_support_event_id`; the code under test is `operations/return_support/relay.py:88-93` and `operations/return_support/ingress.py:92-93`.
- **Rule violated:** rule 10 — a test that passes for a reason other than the property it asserts, with a docstring that states the wrong reason.
- **Confirmed by RV, re-injection.** Removing the length prefixes from **both** `system_entry_id` and `derive_support_event_id` (`"|".join(f"{len(p)}:{p}" …)` → `"|".join(…)`) leaves the whole relevant suite **green: 54 passed, twice.** This is the blind spot the slice reported as closed.
- **Why.** The join uses a `|` separator, and the branch's test inputs contain no `|`. `"a|bc|K|r"` and `"ab|c|K|r"` differ whether or not the parts are length-prefixed — the separator alone disambiguates. I verified this directly: the branch's own case is distinct under *both* implementations, while the case the prefixes actually defend — a part **containing the separator** (`case_id="a|b", support_event_id="c"` vs `case_id="a", support_event_id="b|c"`) — is distinct under the shipped code and **COLLIDES** under the unprefixed one.
- **So the fix moved along the wrong axis.** The previous version's defect was diagnosed as "varied two non-adjacent parts"; the correction made them adjacent. Adjacency is necessary but not sufficient — with a separator present, the collision also requires a part that can contain it. The new docstring states the adjacency reasoning as though it were the whole story, so it now documents a guarantee the test does not check.
- **Severity, honestly.** The underlying code is **correct** — the prefixes are the right design and they hold. And I could not construct a currently-reachable exploitation: for `derive_support_event_id` the varying part an outsider controls (`external_message_id`) is last, and a boundary shift needs the platform-controlled `transport_id` to vary with it; for `system_entry_id` the support-controlled part (`return_record_id`, a support-issued RMA string) is likewise last, behind a constant `entry_kind`. So this is a test-integrity finding, not a live vulnerability. It returns to the slice because the guard is unpinned and the docstring is wrong, and because a later change that reorders those parts would silently remove a defence nothing tests.
- **Fix.** Two lines: put the separator inside a part in both collision tests (`case_id="a|b", support_event_id="c"` vs `case_id="a", support_event_id="b|c"`), and correct the docstrings to say what the prefixes actually defend.

**5. Acceptance 18's proof — strong on the half it covers, and that half is genuinely well made.** Loading the queue against the answer (newest command made oldest-due, so `claim`'s sort offers message three first), driving S2's *real* `IntegrationOutboxDispatcher`, asserting only message one delivered while two and three sit `WAITING_ON_PREDECESSOR` with `status == "RETRY"`, then draining to `dispatcher.dispatched == ids` in chain order — that is a much better instrument than an enqueue-order assertion, and the slice's claim that emptying the predecessor list fails three tests is consistent with what I saw. What it does not cover is whether the store always *produces* a chain, which is F1.

**6. The routing deviation — acceptable, with a named consequence.** §5's requirement is that each stage pin release + `routing_policy_version` + `ordered_candidate_routes[]` **before invocation**, and that attempts be stored with their selected route. The branch does all three: `allowedProviders` in declaration order (not sorted — the release's order is the operator's expressed preference), read from the *currently released* task on each access rather than captured at construction, with the answering provider recorded in the attempt's `detail` beside the pinned candidate. The contract's own gloss on the pin is "which providers were eligible", and that is recorded faithfully. Constraining `StructuredOutputInvoker` to a single route would mean editing shared AI-gateway code outside V2's declared ownership — under rule 11 that would have been the worse choice, and taking a recorded deviation over an ownership breach is the right call. **The consequence worth carrying on the follow-up:** because routing is internal, advancing to "the next pinned candidate" after a `RouteUnavailableError` may re-invoke the same provider, so `block_exhausted` fires after *candidate-count* attempts rather than after providers are genuinely exhausted. That is a retry-budget imprecision, not a determinism hole — the accepted-result rule is unaffected.

**7. Parked lifecycle, limits and the `nl_enabled` switch — correct.** A disabled-NL message is persisted with status `PARKED` and answered **202** with `disposition: PARKED`, never 409; 409 is reserved for the contract's one refusal (same identity, different words). Limits come from released config: 413 over `max_body_characters`, 429 over `rate_window_seconds`, 422 over `max_identifier_characters`, 404 for case access. Parking raises a window-deduped alert (`alert_should_fire`) and escalates past `per_case_quota`. On enable, `drain_parked` runs **on the accept path before the new message is chained**, releasing oldest-first and chaining each released message behind the previously enqueued one — so the backlog genuinely precedes new arrivals rather than merely being enqueued in order. Reading the chain tail from the **outbox** rather than the message store is the right call and correctly reasoned: a parked message has no stream event, and naming one would be `UnknownPredecessorError` at enqueue. (Within a single drain the chaining is sequential and sound; the concurrency exposure is F1's, and it applies to concurrent drains too.)
